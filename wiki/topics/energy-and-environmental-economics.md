# Energy and Environmental Economics

Spring 2024 course, ECON 3381 / ENVR 4931 with graduate sections ECON 5137 / ENVR 5331, taught by Allyssa A. Wadsworth (study guides dated Feb–Apr 2024). Texts: "LT" is Lewis and Tietenberg, *Environmental Economics and Policy* 7e, and "D" is Dahl, *International Energy Markets* 2e — [[sources/uhcl-syllabi-and-course-files]]. Extends [[topics/economics]] (externalities, public goods, surplus) to pollution and electricity markets. Part of [[topics/uhcl-coursework]].

## Course plan (syllabus)
A survey of energy, environmental and natural resource economics: nonmarket valuation, externalities and transportation, climate change, natural resources, coal and gas, oil, and electricity markets. Undergraduates also present one assigned peer-reviewed paper and write two short "Real World Response" essays. The best two of three exams count. The presentation assignment below fits the syllabus's electricity-market weeks (inference).

## Exam format (both guides)
~20 multiple choice, ~10 matching, 1–2 short answers (105/100 points); bring pencil and a basic calculator. A note called `Exams` adds that exams are **more definitions than applied knowledge** (homework is more applied).

## Exam 1 (this section covers LT Chapters 1–2)
The study guide and the syllabus agree that Exam 1 spans LT 1–5 and Dahl 4 and 11, with the syllabus adding LT 12. The later chapters of the guide are not summarized here.
- **Environmental economics** studies how markets and economic models explain and address environmental problems. Chapter 2's conceptual framework: build an economic base, analyze human actions and their consequences, set criteria for desirable outcomes, judge the severity of problems, then design policy.
- **Positive** statements are verifiable (the world as it is); **normative** statements are not (as it should be).
- **Static efficiency**: the chief normative criterion for choosing among simultaneous outcomes; it maximizes surplus from resources.
- **Property rights** define owners' rights and limits (individual, group, state); good ones have **exclusivity, transferability, enforceability**.
- **Negative externality**: a market exchange harms an outside third party (example: a neighbor's barking dog); it shifts the (private) supply curve left relative to social cost and creates **deadweight loss**.
- **Surplus**: consumer surplus (above price), producer surplus (below price), total surplus, and DWL.
- **Types of goods** (rival × excludable): private (cell phone), club (movie theater), common (water, fish), public (national defense). Same framework as [[topics/economics]] Chapter 11.

## Exam 2 (LT Chapter 13 and beyond; water pollution)
**Conflict:** the syllabus lists Exam 2 as LT 8–12 (mostly climate change), Dahl 14, 3, 4 and 8. The study guide `Exam-2-Review` has sections for LT 13, Dahl 14, 4, 3 and 8, with no LT 8–12. The syllabus says its schedule can change, so the course may have moved, but nothing in the vault confirms that. The syllabus also files LT 13 under natural resource economics, not water pollution.

- Why water pollution is distinctive: recreational benefits matter more than for air; large economies of scale make centralized treatment plants possible; sources are hard to trace.
- **Receiving waters**: surface water (rivers, lakes, oceans), groundwater (once thought pristine, now contaminated), oceans (oil spills, dumping, trash).
- **Point** vs **nonpoint** sources: nonpoint (pesticide runoff) is harder to control and makes up **over half** of the waste load on US waters.
- **Fund pollutants** (the environment can assimilate them if absorption exceeds discharge): degradable organics, thermal pollution (power plants returning heated water), eutrophication (excess nitrogen and phosphorus feeding algae).
- **Stock pollutants** accumulate with no natural removal: cadmium, lead, mercury. Case: mercury dumping in Japan accumulated in fish, with 52 deaths and 150+ cases of nerve damage.

## Electricity-market presentation (April 2024)
- Assignment (`Presentation – 4/24 Due`): a 10–15 minute talk on electricity markets with a one-slide methodology.
- Notes (`Presentation-notes`) summarize a study comparing **zonal vs nodal** pricing in Texas (ERCOT): daily CO₂ emissions rose 5.5% (a negative externality); zonal pricing gave strong incentives to game the system; under **nodal** pricing coal operating costs and CO₂ rose, while natural gas changed less and gas-fired **combined-cycle** units reduced costs on low-demand days; natural gas generation tracks net-load ramping because it is load-following (net load = ERCOT load minus wind and nuclear).
