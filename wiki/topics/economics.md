# Economics

Notes from UHCL **ECON 2301 (Principles of Macroeconomics)** and **ECON 2302 (Principles of Microeconomics)**, plus exam notes. Part of [[topics/uhcl-coursework]]. Quantitative overlap with [[topics/math-1324-business-math]] (functions, linear models) and [[topics/statistics]]. Opportunity-cost thinking recurs in [[topics/corporate-finance]] and [[topics/managerial-accounting]] (differential analysis).

## Core concepts

### Foundations (ECON 2301)
- **Economics**: how individuals, firms and society allocate limited resources among competing wants. *Microeconomics* covers individual/business choices and markets; *macroeconomics* covers inflation, unemployment and growth.
- **Three basic questions**: what, how, and for whom goods and services are produced.
- **Key principles** (the notes list eight): scarcity forces choices; true cost is *opportunity cost*; specialization yields gains; people respond to incentives; "how much" is a marginal decision (do it if MB > MC, optimum at MB = MC); markets are generally efficient but can fail; growth/low unemployment/low inflation don't always coincide; institutions and creativity explain wealth.
- **Opportunity cost** is treated as a ratio (what you give up / what you gain), and rises as production of a good expands.
- **Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)**: combinations of two goods producible at full employment; points on it are production-efficient; outward shifts = growth (more resources or better technology). "Free lunch" = getting something without giving anything up.
- **Specialization and trade**: *absolute* vs *comparative* advantage (lower opportunity cost). Worked example with Liz and Joe (smoothies vs salads): trade at 1 smoothie for 2 salads puts both outside their own PPFs.
- **Other distinctions**: efficiency vs equity; positive (testable) vs normative (opinion) statements; models and *ceteris paribus*; factors of production (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability).

### Supply and demand (ECON 2302, Ch. 4)
- **Competitive market**: many buyers/sellers, each a price taker.
- **Law of demand** and **law of supply**; market curves are horizontal sums of individual curves (worked examples with lattes: Sam + Dean for demand, Starbucks + Peet's for supply).
- **Demand shifters**: number of buyers, income (normal vs inferior goods), prices of substitutes/complements, tastes, expectations. **Supply shifters**: input prices, technology, number of sellers, expectations. A change in the good's own price is a *movement along* the curve, not a shift.
- **Equilibrium**: Qs = Qd. Price above equilibrium creates a surplus (price falls); below creates a shortage (price rises). Example in notes: equilibrium of $3.00 and 15 units.
- **Three-step method** for analyzing events: identify which curve shifts, which direction, then compare old and new equilibrium.
- Prices act as signals that allocate scarce resources.

### Elasticity (ECON 2302, Ch. 5)
- **Price elasticity of demand** = %ΔQd / %ΔP. Determinants: availability of close substitutes, necessity vs luxury, how narrowly the market is defined, and time horizon (more elastic over time).
- **Midpoint method** removes direction-dependence (example: $4↔$6 and 120↔80 units gives elasticity of 1 either way).
- **Interpretation**: elastic (>1), inelastic (<1), unit elastic (=1). Flatter curve = more elastic.
- **Total revenue test**: inelastic demand means price and revenue move together; elastic means they move in opposite directions; unit elastic leaves revenue unchanged.
- **Other elasticities**: income elasticity (normal goods positive, inferior negative; Engel's Law), cross-price elasticity (positive for substitutes, negative for complements).
- **Elasticity along a linear demand curve**: the slope is constant but elasticity is not. At high prices and low quantities demand is **elastic**; at low prices and high quantities it is **inelastic**; elasticity is exactly 1 at the midpoint (the revenue-maximizing point). In the notes' table (price 7 down to 1, quantity 0 to 12) elasticity falls 13, 3.7, 1.8, 1, 0.6, 0.3, 0.1 and total revenue peaks at 24 between prices 4 and 3 (recomputed with the midpoint method: all values check out). A constant elasticity is never possible for a linear demand curve.
- **Elasticity of supply**: = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP; depends on seller flexibility and time; more elastic in the long run (beachfront land vs manufactured books). Worked example: milk rising from $2.85 to $3.15 raises output from 9,000 to 11,000 gallons; midpoint changes are 10% and 20%, so the elasticity is **2** (recomputed).
- **Chapter 5 summary** (`5-4 Conclusion`): demand is more elastic with close substitutes, luxuries, narrowly defined markets and more time; total revenue moves with price when demand is inelastic and against it when elastic; the same tools are then applied to wheat, oil and illegal drugs.

### Public goods and common resources (Ch. 11)
- Goods classified by **excludability** and **rivalry**: private, public, common resource, club good. Road example: whether it is a club good, private good, public good or common resource depends on tolling and congestion.
- **Free-rider problem** explains why markets under-supply public goods; cost-benefit analysis is the (imprecise) tool for deciding provision.
- **Tragedy of the commons** (sheep on shared land) arises from a negative externality; remedies: corrective taxes, regulation, permit auctions, or privatizing/dividing the resource.

### Costs and competitive firms (ECON 2302, Chapters 13–14)
- **Economic vs accounting profit**: both are revenue minus cost, but economists count **explicit + implicit** costs (forgone wages, forgone interest) while accountants count explicit only, so accounting profit is usually larger.
- **Production function and diminishing marginal product**: in the stand example (a $10 fixed "cost of stand"), output 0/20/35/45/50 for 0–4 workers gives marginal products 20, 15, 10, 5. With a $10 fixed cost and $15 per worker, total cost rises 10, 25, 40, 55, 70.
- **Cost measures**: TC = FC + VC; ATC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ. In the 10-unit table (FC = 5), ATC bottoms out at **2.5** at Q = 4–5 where MC = 2.5 (the **efficient scale**); MC below ATC pulls ATC down, MC above pulls it up. Short run = some fixed input; long run = all variable, with economies, constant returns or diseconomies of scale.
- **Competitive firm**: price taker with AR = MR = P. **Profit-maximizing rule: produce where MR = MC.** Short-run **shutdown** if P < AVC (fixed costs are sunk and irrelevant); long-run **exit** if P < ATC. The firm's short-run supply curve is its MC curve above AVC.
- **Long run**: entry when profits are positive, exit when losses; equilibrium has **zero economic profit** with **P = MC = minimum ATC** (accounting profit can still be positive because implicit costs are counted). The long-run supply curve is horizontal if firms have identical, unchanging costs, and upward-sloping otherwise. A competitive equilibrium is efficient because P = MC.

### Market structures: monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly (ECON 2302, later chapters)
These notes are filed under misleading names (`Chapter 15 notes`, `Chapter 16-17`), which collide with the accounting chapter numbers in [[topics/managerial-accounting]].
- **Monopoly**: sole seller with market power, arising from barriers to entry (key-resource ownership, government grant, natural monopoly). Because it faces the whole market demand curve, **MR < P** (output effect vs price effect). Profit-maximizes where **MR = MC**, then reads price off the demand curve; profit = (P − ATC) × Q. It has **no supply curve** (price maker). Result: P > MC and a **deadweight loss**.
- **Price discrimination**: charging different buyers different prices based on willingness to pay (movie discounts, coupons, need-based aid, quantity discounts); perfect discrimination captures all surplus with no deadweight loss.
- **Policy toward monopoly**: antitrust (Sherman 1890, Clayton 1914), regulation (marginal-cost pricing loses money for natural monopolies, so subsidize or set P = ATC), public ownership, or do nothing.
- **Monopolistic competition**: many sellers, differentiated products, free entry; zero long-run profit but P > MC and excess capacity (output left of minimum ATC). Inefficiencies include markup, product-variety and business-stealing externalities. **Advertising** and **brand names** debated (manipulation/impeding competition vs information/quality signal).
- **Oligopoly**: few sellers, strategic behavior, **game theory**. Collusion/cartels are unstable (each firm has an incentive to cheat); **Nash equilibrium** gives output between monopoly and competitive levels; the **prisoner's dilemma** and **dominant strategy** explain failed cooperation; repeated games allow tit-for-tat. **Antitrust controversies**: resale price maintenance, predatory pricing, tying.
- **Concentration ratio** = share of output from the four largest firms.
