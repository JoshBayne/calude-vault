## Terms to Know
1. Ad Populum Fallacy
	1. Fallacy where something is asserted and others assert it or believe it as well.  *Why? Because thats what we do.* Domination by consent, hegemonic by its nature. Shows wht a culture or person values, but has ot been crtically thought about.
2. Connotative Meaning
	1. Cloud of ideas suggested by a word or phrase. Collective understanding. Finding ways to connect to the audience in an immediate manner
3. Analogical Reasoning
	1. Type of reasoning that encourages an audience to embrace a conclusion because they connect by broad experience. Looking for commonality and solidarity. Used to find ways to connect to people *Its like this thing we all know.* Verbal equivalent of a politician wearing jeans and rolling their sleeves up.
4. Denotative Meaning
	1. Literal meaning of a word or phrase. Dictionary meaning. Must be as clear as possible with meaning. About honoring jargon.
5. Fallacy
	1. Weakness in logic
6. Communication
7. Impassioned Publc Speaking
	1. Results from a series of increasingly negative events stemming from one negative experience or event. Can cause weak causal reasoning. Stereotyping is an example. Limitation is the fact that it is hard to recover from due to the personal nature. Important because people seek solidarity. 
8. Hasty Generalization Fallacy
	1. Type of fallacy that draws a conclusion based on inadequate evidence. The asshole fallacy. Limitation of this fallacy means you always act from emotion. Important because its often a first response, leading with emotion can interfere with logic.
9. Rhetorical Frame
	1. Way a speaker focuses their words in a relationship for immediate needs. Shows you who you are, even when you arent thinking about it, as a public speaker. Using all the elements around you to your benefit for an effective message. *Passion is always connected to our rhetorical strategy.*
10. Slippery Slope Fallacy
	1. Results from a series of increasingly negative events stemming from one negative experience or event. Can cause weak causal reasoning. Stereotyping is an example. Limitation is the fact that it is hard to recover from due to the personal nature. Important because people seek solidarity. 
11. Communication As Representation
	1. Words mean things
12. Communication As Constitutive
	1. Communication shapes and creates social reality
13. Advocacy
	1. Active participation and care (has to have an *active* participation, cannot be passive)
14. Social Significance
	1. Relevant or meaningful for a culture or group
15. Dialogic
	1. Describes when two or more people are engaged in a mutual sharing of ideas.
16. Praxis
	1. Defined by Freire (2005) as “reflection and action on the world in order to transform it.”
17. Communication Anxiety
	1. A feeling of aversion to engag-ing in communication with others; for many people, this feeling of aversion is specific to a certain type of com-munication, such as speaking in public to an assembled audience.
18. Monologic
	1. Communication that suggests that only one person's voice is present and matters.
19. Ethic of Care
	1. The idea that efforts at advocacy must be informed by a dialogue and relationship with the people who will be affected by communication. In adopting the premise that one should care for others with commu-nication, one hopes to emphasize the importance of engaging with others to learn more about them and oneself.
20. Implicit Bias
	1.  An unreflective or unconscious belief or stereotype about a particular group of people.
21. Epidictic Speech
	1. Speeches that address events in the present. In this way, such speeches attempt to show how we currently stand in relation to one another in the community. Good examples of epideictic speeches include toasts at weddings (which highlight bonds of family and friendship) and award nomination and acceptance speeches (which establish the worthiness of those receiving or presenting an award).
22. Persuasive Speech
	1. A speech in which the speaker’s primary purpose is to convince audience members to take a particular action.
23. Change the Focus
	1. Involves deliberately disrupting an audience’s sense that they know what one will say and do next. A sense that they will only get what they expect can dull audience members’ attention and lead them to listen, look, and think less carefully. 
24. Change the Frame
	1. A way of changing the focus that involves altering how an audience filters out some communication or treats it as less relevant than other communication. For instance, a person’s perspective might change depending on if they were told an agitat-ed professor had a family member hospitalized, versus if they were told that agitated professor was new to campus and teaching their first class.
25. Bias
	1. Distortion of reality in communication based on one’s assumptions or beliefs.
26. Ephemera
	1. An idea or process that only exists for a very short time frame.
27. Lay Testimony
	1. The perspective of the average person who is affected by the issue at hand. For example, to better explain the issue of why faculty members might choose to go on strike, one might include information from an interview with faculty members about how they are affected by a lack of cost-of-living salary increase.
28. Public Seaking Anxiety
	1. A discomfort associated with speaking before a large group of people.
29. Contextualize
	1. To provide meaningful background and in-terpretations for sources when you cite from them.
