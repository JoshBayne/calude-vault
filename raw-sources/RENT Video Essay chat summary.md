# RENT — Video Essay Summary

**Working title:** "No Day But Today: Why RENT Doesn't Hold Up" **Thesis (your position):** The main characters act entitled and spoiled. The show exploits the AIDS epidemic. The show is overrated and under-hated. **Target runtime:** 32–36 minutes **Status:** Framework done. Sources listed below. Some items need a check before you script (see the last section).

## 1. Decisions from the committee and your answers

- **Entitlement is the main claim.** It gets the most runtime (Act 2, about 9.5 minutes).
- **Larson's death is a recurring device.** Jonathan Larson died the night before the first full Off-Broadway preview. This makes the show hard to criticize. Return to it three times. Each return is heavier than the last (brief, then longer, then the emotional close).
- **Steelman before you rebut.** Each major claim gets a fair version of the defense first.
- **Fix the "underhated" claim.** Serious critics hated the show from day one (Brustein, 1996). Fan culture buried that criticism. Do not say that nobody criticized RENT.
- **Keep "exploitative" precise.** The strong version is about erasure and narrow representation (whose AIDS story gets told). It is not an accusation that Larson acted in bad faith.
- **The close must concede something real.** RENT put gay, HIV-positive, addicted, and poor characters in front of a mass audience at a time when that was rare. Without this concession, the essay looks one-sided.

## 3. Sources used

These claims came from web searches. Source names are as the search results showed them. I did not keep the full URLs.

**Stature and Larson**

- RENT won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama and Tony Awards, including Best Musical. It ran 12 years on Broadway (10th longest-running show at the time of the source). (Encyclopedia.com, Pulitzer.org.)
- Larson died of an aortic dissection the night before the first full Off-Broadway performance. Press coverage focused on the irony of the timing before critics had judged the show itself.

**Entitlement critique**

- **Robert Brustein, The New Republic, 1996.** He called the characters examples of sentimental, lifestyle-obsessed posturing. He said the lyrics replace craft with raw emotion.
- **America Magazine review.** What some see as a critique of capitalism, others hear as entitled twenty-somethings: "Peter Pans with tattoos."
- **TheaterMania point-counterpoint.** Mark is an upper-middle-class man from Scarsdale. He could pay rent if he answered his mother's calls. The conflict is two white "artists" who feel entitled to bilk their Black landlord (Benny).
- **Chapter-by-chapter script read-through (fan blog).** Mark and Roger never think of a paying job. Use as a reader's reaction, not as authority.

**AIDS critique**

- RENT was seen as new because it put the AIDS crisis in a mainstream Broadway musical at a scale that earlier plays did not reach. (Used for the steelman.)
- **Princeton Commons**, "No Day But Today: Theatre, Gay America, and the Theatrical Sanitation of AIDS 1985–1996." _The Normal Heart_, _Angels in America_, _Falsettos_, and _RENT_ leave out protagonists of color, queer women, and people in real poverty. RENT's narrator is a white straight man with financial security.
- Critics ask why Angel, the most effeminate gay character, is the only one of four HIV-positive characters who dies on stage.
- **Sarah Schulman**, _Stagestruck: Theater, AIDS, and the Marketing of Gay America._ She alleges that Larson took the plot from her work. **Mark this as contested on screen.**
- **Windy City Times** reflection piece. It calls the show "AIDS: The Musical" and says it asks the audience to love people with AIDS without engaging memory or thought. This is opinion journalism. Label it that way.

**Reassessment**

- **The Billfold**, 20th-anniversary retrospective. It calls Maureen "a narcissistic monster without a leg of talent to stand on." It calls "Your Eyes" one of the weakest songs. It says the culture recognized the characters as self-indulgent.
