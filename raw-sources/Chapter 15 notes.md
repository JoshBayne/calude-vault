Monopoly
- a firm that is the sole seller of a product without close subs
- has market power
	- The ability to influence the market price of the product it sells
	- a competitive firm has no market power
- Arise due to barriers to entry
	- Other firms cannot enter the market to compete with it

Three barriers to entry
1. Monopoly on resources
	1. A single firm owns a key resource
2. Government regultion
	1. The govt gives a single firm the exclusive right to produce a good
3. Production process
	1. natural monoply - a single firm can produce the entire market Q at lower cost than could several firms

Monopoly vs Competition
In a comp maret the market demand curve slopes downward. Bt the demand curve for any individual firms product is horizontal at the market price the firm can increase Q without lowering P, so MR = P for the competitive firm
A monopolist is the only seller so it faces the entire demand curve. to Sell a larger Q it must reduce P. Thus MR is not equal to P

Understnding the onopolsts MR
- Increaseing Q has two effects on revenue:
	- Output effect: higher output raises revenue
	- price effect: lower price reduces revenue
- Marginal revenue, MR < P
	- to sell a larger Q, the monopolist must reduce the price on all units it sells
	- Is negative if price effect > output effect

Profit maximization
- Like a competitive market, a monopolist maximizes profit by producing the quantity where MR = MC
	- sets the highest price consumers are willing to pay for that quantity
	- It finds this price from the D curve
	- As with a competitive firm, the monopolists pofit equals
		- (P - ATC) x Q

A monopoly does not have a supply curve
- A competitive firm takes P as a given
	- has a supply curve that shows how its Q depends on P
- A monopoly firm is a "price maker"
	- Q does not depend on P
	- Q and  are jointly determined by MC, MR, and the demand curve
	- hence no supply curve for monopoly

The welfare cost of monopoly
- recall
	- competitive market equilibium P = MC and total surplus maximized
- Monopoly equilibrium, P > MR = MC
	- the value to buyers of an additional unit P exceeds the cost of the resources needed to produce that unit MC
	- The monopoly Q too low - could increase tota surplus with a larger Q
	- Monopoly results in deadweight loss

Price discrimination
Sell the same good at different prices to different buyers
A firm can increase profit by charging a higher price to buyers with higher wilingness to pay
requires the ability to seperate customers according to their willingness to pay
can raise economic welfare

Perfect price discrimination
charge each customer a different price
monopoly firm gets the entire surplus
no deadweight loss

Price discrimination in the real world
- Perfect price descrimination not possible in real world
	- no firm knows every buyers WTP
	- Buyers do not real it to sellers
- Price discrimination
	- firms divide customers into groups based on some observable trait that is likely related to willingness to pay, such as age
- Examples
	- Movie ticket discounts
		- discounts for seniors, students, and people who go to matinee showtimes
			- lower WTP than people who pay full price on friday night
	- Discount coupons
		- people who have time to clip and organize coupons are more likely to have lower income and lower WTP
	- need based financial aid
		- low income families have lower wtp for college
	- quantity disconts
		- a buyers WTP declines with additional units, so firms charge less per unit for large quantities than for small quantities

Prevalence of Monopoly
- Pure monopoly - rare in real world
- many firms have market power due to:
	- Sell a unique variety of a product
	- have a large market share and few significant competitors
- In many such cases most of the results from this chapter apply including:
	- Markup of price over marginal cost
	- deadweight loss
